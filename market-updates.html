What's actually moving the manufacturing insurance market

A curated digest of the trends and headlines shaping rates, capacity, and coverage decisions for manufacturing accounts — each entry links to its source so you can dig deeper before a renewal conversation.

Last curated: September 28, 2026. Each entry below is a deeper dive, not just a headline — the goal is enough detail to actually use in a renewal conversation. Always confirm current figures with your carrier or wholesaler.

Property Updated Sept 28, 2026 · Q2 2026 data

Property softening just hit a fifth straight quarter — the calmest commercial market since 2017, but rebuild costs haven't gotten the memo

The property soft market isn't leveling off — it's deepening. Commercial property pricing fell 7.1% in Q1 2026, the steepest quarterly drop on record at the time, and then fell another 8.1% in Q2 — the fifth consecutive quarterly decrease. Zoom out and overall commercial insurance pricing (all lines blended) rose just 0.2% through the first half of 2026, the calmest the market has been since 2017. Analysts now expect full-year P&C premium growth to slow to roughly 3% in 2026, down from 5.5% in 2025. Clean risks outside catastrophe zones are seeing cuts up to 10%; cat-exposed accounts are seeing real but smaller relief.

The catch: replacement cost is still climbing

Commercial reconstruction costs rose 4.4% year-over-year nationally, with some states running above 7% — meaning premium and replacement cost are now moving in opposite directions. A rate quote that looks like a win on the declarations page can still leave a plant underinsured if the statement of values wasn't updated to match (see the Tariffs entry below for why rebuild costs specifically are still under pressure).

Why it matters: This is a genuinely strong renewal story to bring a manufacturing client — five straight quarters of softening is a real, usable data point. But don't let a falling rate substitute for an updated statement of values. A lower premium on an outdated building/equipment valuation just means the coinsurance penalty (Lesson 2) is quietly getting worse even as the bill gets smaller.
Sources: Insurance Business, "US property market outlook 2026: Competitive rates and emerging opportunities"
   Beancount, "Commercial Property Insurance Just Got Cheaper" (Sept 9, 2026)
Casualty & Auto Updated Sept 28, 2026 · August 2026 data

Casualty hardening is decelerating industry-wide — but commercial auto is still the one line moving the wrong way

The broad casualty trend has shifted from "still hardening" to "hardening, but slowing." WTW's Commercial Lines Insurance Pricing Survey shows overall U.S. commercial rates up just 0.5% in Q2 2026, down sharply from +2.5% in Q1 and +3.8% in the same quarter last year. The deceleration is holding into the latest monthly data: the Ivans Index puts general liability at 4.18% in August, down from 4.99% in July — a second straight month of easing. Large accounts saw an overall price decrease for the first time since 2017.

Auto is the exception, not the rule

Commercial auto rose 5.8% in early 2026 — the only major line that went up while overall commercial premiums fell 1.2% in that same snapshot. The same August Ivans data shows some cooling within the trend — auto rates at 3.58%, down from 4.03% in July — but auto remains the outlier still moving up while most of the market moves down. The underlying drivers haven't changed: a persistent shortage of qualified commercial drivers, rising bodily injury claim costs, and the litigation dynamics covered in the next entry.

Why it matters: The "casualty is getting worse every quarter" story from earlier in 2026 needs an update — broad casualty hardening is decelerating, which is a real, current data point for a renewal conversation. But don't let that soften your auto conversation: it's still the one line bucking the trend, and it's the line most manufacturers with a delivery or field-service fleet actually carry meaningful exposure in.
Sources: Insurance Journal, "WTW, Ivans Reports Signal More Commercial Rate Moderation" (Sept 14, 2026)
   Beinsure, "US commercial insurance prices rise 0.5% in Q2 2026, WTW says"
Casualty & Litigation Updated Sept 21, 2026 · Q2 2026 verdicts

New Q2 2026 verdicts show litigation funding pushing awards past primary limits and into the umbrella tower

"Nuclear verdict" is industry shorthand for a jury award of $10 million or more (a "thermonuclear" verdict tops $100 million). The full-year 2025 trend was already stark — verdicts up 40.7%, with 149 verdicts totaling $25.1 billion across 28 states, and 68 industries touched in 2026 (up from 55 in 2024) — and fresh Q2 2026 case data shows the pattern accelerating, not correcting.

Two verdicts from this year illustrate the scale

  • A jury awarded the family of a woman killed by a counterfeit airbag a combined $603 million ($360M punitive + $243M compensatory) against a China-based manufacturer.
  • A Livingston County jury found a motorcycle's brake system defective and awarded $120 million in punitive damages to each of two plaintiffs, for a total verdict over $287 million.
  • Analysts specifically flag third-party litigation funding as an accelerant — it's associated with longer settlement times and higher-severity outcomes, with verdicts "increasingly piercing primary limits and reaching into excess and umbrella coverage."
Why it matters: "Piercing primary limits and reaching into excess and umbrella coverage" is a direct, quotable reason to review tower adequacy (Lesson 8) — this isn't hypothetical, it's what carriers and analysts are seeing happen in verdicts handed down this year. A primary GL limit of $1–2 million is not a meaningful defense against a $287M or $603M verdict; the umbrella/excess tower is doing all the real work, and it needs to be sized for that reality.
Sources: Enlyte, "The Largest Nuclear Verdicts in Q2 2026: Social Inflation Continues"
   Insurance Journal, "Nuclear Verdicts Go Boom, Increase 40.7% in 2025"
Workers' Comp Updated Sept 28, 2026 · WA 2027 rate proposed Sept 22

A second state follows California's lead — Washington proposes a 2027 rate hike, and the "favorable cycle has limits" story is now a pattern, not a one-off

Workers' comp remains the industry's best-performing line, with a national combined ratio near 91% for a twelfth consecutive year, and manufacturing keeps outperforming even that: Connecticut's 2026 NCCI filing shows manufacturing specifically getting a -5.6% loss cost reduction. But two state regulators have now acted in the same direction within a month of each other: California's commissioner took action effective September 1, 2026 to stabilize a market facing rising costs, and on September 22 Washington's Department of Labor & Industries proposed a 4.9% average rate increase for 2027 — roughly $1.44/week more per full-time employee — explicitly to keep pace with rising wage-replacement and medical costs.

What's prompting regulators to step in

  • Cumulative trauma claims — repetitive stress injuries and musculoskeletal disorders built up over time rather than a single incident — are named as a specific, growing pressure on reserves in both states' reasoning.
  • Rising medical treatment and medical-legal costs, plus escalating claims-adjusting costs, are causing deteriorating accident-year combined ratios even while the headline combined ratio still looks strong.
  • Frequency is still falling industry-wide (robotics, automated safety sensors, stronger OSHA compliance) — the good news hasn't reversed, but severity and claim complexity are clearly the part of the story regulators in multiple states are now actively managing rather than letting ride.
Why it matters: One state acting could be a local quirk; two states acting inside the same month, for the same stated reasons, is a trend worth naming out loud to clients. Manufacturing is still in a genuinely favorable position on workers' comp, but don't present "rates keep falling" as a permanent condition — for clients in states with growing cumulative-trauma exposure (older workforce, repetitive-motion roles), return-to-work programs and ergonomics investment are the lever that keeps a plant on the good side of this shift.
Sources: Washington State L&I, "Proposed 2027 workers' comp rate increase helps state keep up with rising costs" (Sept 22, 2026)
   California Department of Insurance, "Commissioner Lara takes action to maintain stable workers' compensation market amid rising costs"
Cyber Updated Sept 28, 2026 · Jan–Jul 2026 data

Manufacturing ransomware has already blown past all of 2024 — and Black Kite's full report confirms it's the #1 target for a 4th straight year

Manufacturing remains the single most-targeted sector for ransomware globally, and the 2026 pace is outrunning last year's record. Tracked incidents against manufacturers hit 1,183 victims through just the first seven months of 2026 — up 39.7% year-over-year and already surpassing the full-year total for 2024. Black Kite's full 2026 report confirms manufacturing ranks #1 for the fourth consecutive year, accounting for 22% of all disclosed ransomware victims across every industry tracked. The geographic mix is shifting too: the U.S. share of attacks fell from 52% to 35% as European targets grew 85% (412 US attacks vs. 369 in Europe this period, with the rest of the world nearly doubling to 402) — this is now a genuinely global problem, not a US-concentrated one.

The threat actor landscape has shifted since last update

  • Current leaderboard: Qilin, followed by "The Gentlemen" — a group that only surfaced in September 2025 and has already claimed 142 victims, the #2 spot — then Akira, DragonForce, and INC Ransom. The speed at which a brand-new group climbed to #2 shows how low the barrier to entry has become.
  • A live example this month: Aebi Schmidt, a Switzerland-based manufacturer of airport and road maintenance vehicles, had operations disrupted after a ransomware infection — a concrete, current illustration that this isn't an abstract statistic.
  • Ransomware groups are increasingly exploiting the operational shutdown itself as leverage — the disruption to a manufacturer's supply chain is the pressure point, not just the threat of leaked data.
Why it matters: If a client asks "does this really happen to companies like us," Aebi Schmidt is this month's answer, not a hypothetical. Confirm the policy's business interruption trigger explicitly covers OT/production outages (not only IT data breaches), and don't assume last year's threat-actor briefing is still accurate — the group most likely to hit a given account has probably changed since you last checked.
Sources: Industrial Cyber, "Black Kite: Manufacturing ransomware surged in 2026, spreading beyond the US"
   Cybersecurity Insiders, "Manufacturing Ransomware Attacks Surge Nearly 40% as Mid-Market and European Victims Rise"
Tariffs & Supply Chain Updated Sept 28, 2026 · Canada tariffs effective Sept 8

60% of U.S. aluminum imports come from Canada — which is exactly the supply the new tariff war just hit hardest

The tariff picture that was already pushing up replacement costs earlier this year has escalated further, and this week's reporting puts a sharper number on exactly how exposed U.S. manufacturers are. Since April 6, 2026, amended Section 232 tariffs impose a 50% tariff on raw copper, steel, and aluminum, a flat 25% tariff on derivative products containing more than 15% covered metal, and a reduced 10% tariff on derivative products made using U.S.-origin metal. Effective September 8, 2026, Canada raised its own counter-tariffs from 25% to 50%, matching the U.S. rate, covering more than 300 U.S. steel and aluminum products. The reason this specific fight matters so much for manufacturers: Canada supplied 60% of all U.S. unwrought aluminum imports in the first half of 2026 — there is no easy alternate source at that volume.

Why this keeps being a live claims problem, not just a cost story

Most manufacturing property policies carry an 80%, 90%, or 100% coinsurance requirement (see Lesson 2). Every time the metals tariff structure changes — and it has now changed materially twice this year — a plant's true replacement cost moves again, while the declared value on file typically doesn't move until the next renewal. Reporting this week also flags that because U.S.-Canada steel and aluminum supply chains are so deeply integrated, materials can effectively get taxed more than once as components cross the border multiple times during processing — a compounding effect that's easy to underestimate if you're only pricing the headline tariff rate.

Why it matters: A manufacturer sourcing aluminum from Canada isn't dealing with a marginal cost increase — they're dealing with their primary supply source getting 50% more expensive at the same time cross-border processing can stack additional tariff hits. That combination makes this quarter's statement-of-values conversation urgent, not routine, and it's also a direct reason to revisit supplier diversification and contingent business interruption coverage (Lesson 6) if that Canadian aluminum dependency runs through a key supplier rather than the manufacturer's own imports.
Sources: OilPrice.com, "Canada Tariffs Deepen North America's Steel and Aluminum Squeeze"
