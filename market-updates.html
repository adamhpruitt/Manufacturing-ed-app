What's actually moving the manufacturing insurance market

A curated digest of the trends and headlines shaping rates, capacity, and coverage decisions for manufacturing accounts — each entry links to its source so you can dig deeper before a renewal conversation.

Last curated: October 5, 2026. Each entry below is a deeper dive, not just a headline — the goal is enough detail to actually use in a renewal conversation. Always confirm current figures with your carrier or wholesaler.

Property Updated Oct 5, 2026 · Willis Q2 2026 report

Large-account property rates just posted their steepest drop in a decade — and Willis says more is coming

The property soft market isn't leveling off — it's deepening, and it's deepest where accounts are largest. Broad-market commercial property pricing fell 7.1% in Q1 2026 and another 8.1% in Q2 (a fifth consecutive quarterly decrease), but Willis's Q2 report shows large and complex North American property accounts fell an average of 14.5% — the steepest drop in a decade, moving pricing back toward levels last seen in 2019. Willis is now forecasting further declines: -5% to -15% for single-carrier property programs and -15% to -25% for shared/layered placements at upcoming renewals.

The catch: replacement cost is still climbing

Commercial reconstruction costs rose 4.4% year-over-year nationally, with some states running above 7% — meaning premium and replacement cost are now moving in opposite directions. A rate quote that looks like a win on the declarations page can still leave a plant underinsured if the statement of values wasn't updated to match (see the Tariffs entry below for why rebuild costs specifically are still under pressure).

Why it matters: If a manufacturing client runs a large or complex property program (multiple locations, higher TIV, layered/shared placement), the relief coming at their next renewal could be meaningfully bigger than the broad-market headline number — this is worth quantifying specifically rather than quoting the generic "property is soft" line. But the coinsurance warning doesn't go away just because the discount is bigger: a 15–25% rate cut on an outdated replacement cost valuation can still leave real exposure on the table.
Sources: Artemis.bm, "NA commercial property insurance rates fall the most in a decade, hard market reverses: Willis"
   Insurance Business, "Large property rates fall 14.5% as excess casualty increases show signs of peaking"
Casualty & Auto Updated Oct 5, 2026 · Willis Q2 2026 report

Casualty hardening is decelerating industry-wide — and Willis now says the excess casualty increases are showing signs of peaking

The broad casualty trend has shifted from "still hardening" to "hardening, but slowing." WTW's Commercial Lines Insurance Pricing Survey shows overall U.S. commercial rates up just 0.5% in Q2 2026, down sharply from +2.5% in Q1 and +3.8% in the same quarter last year. The deceleration is holding into the latest monthly data: the Ivans Index puts general liability at 4.18% in August, down from 4.99% in July — a second straight month of easing. The same Willis report driving this week's property headline (see the Property entry above) carries an important companion finding: excess casualty rate increases are showing signs of peaking — the first language of its kind from a major broker on this line in some time. Large accounts saw an overall price decrease for the first time since 2017.

Auto is the exception, not the rule

Commercial auto rose 5.8% in early 2026 — the only major line that went up while overall commercial premiums fell 1.2% in that same snapshot. The same August Ivans data shows some cooling within the trend — auto rates at 3.58%, down from 4.03% in July — but auto remains the outlier still moving up while most of the market moves down. The underlying drivers haven't changed: a persistent shortage of qualified commercial drivers, rising bodily injury claim costs, and the litigation dynamics covered in the next entry.

Why it matters: "Signs of peaking" is not the same as "falling" — don't oversell this as a casualty soft market. But it is a real shift in tone from brokers who spent several years saying the opposite, and it's worth flagging to a manufacturing client who's been bracing for another double-digit umbrella/excess increase: the ceiling may finally be in sight, even if the floor isn't here yet. Don't let that soften your auto conversation, though: it's still the one line bucking the trend, and it's the line most manufacturers with a delivery or field-service fleet actually carry meaningful exposure in.
Sources: Insurance Business, "Large property rates fall 14.5% as excess casualty increases show signs of peaking, Willis finds"
   Insurance Journal, "WTW, Ivans Reports Signal More Commercial Rate Moderation" (Sept 14, 2026)
Casualty & Litigation Updated Oct 5, 2026 · Q2 2026 verdicts

New Q2 2026 verdicts show litigation funding pushing awards past primary limits and into the umbrella tower

"Nuclear verdict" is industry shorthand for a jury award of $10 million or more (a "thermonuclear" verdict tops $100 million). The full-year 2025 trend was already stark — verdicts up 40.7%, with continued tracking now putting the total at nearly 200 verdicts totaling more than $25.6 billion against corporate defendants across 28 states, and 68 industries touched in 2026 (up from 55 in 2024) — and fresh Q2 2026 case data shows the pattern accelerating, not correcting.

Two verdicts from this year illustrate the scale

  • A jury awarded the family of a woman killed by a counterfeit airbag a combined $603 million ($360M punitive + $243M compensatory) against a China-based manufacturer.
  • A Livingston County jury found a motorcycle's brake system defective and awarded $120 million in punitive damages to each of two plaintiffs, for a total verdict over $287 million.
  • Analysts specifically flag third-party litigation funding as an accelerant — it's associated with longer settlement times and higher-severity outcomes, with verdicts "increasingly piercing primary limits and reaching into excess and umbrella coverage."
Why it matters: "Piercing primary limits and reaching into excess and umbrella coverage" is a direct, quotable reason to review tower adequacy (Lesson 8) — this isn't hypothetical, it's what carriers and analysts are seeing happen in verdicts handed down this year. A primary GL limit of $1–2 million is not a meaningful defense against a $287M or $603M verdict; the umbrella/excess tower is doing all the real work, and it needs to be sized for that reality.
Sources: Enlyte, "The Largest Nuclear Verdicts in Q2 2026: Social Inflation Continues"
   Insurance Journal, "Nuclear Verdicts Go Boom, Increase 40.7% in 2025"
Workers' Comp Updated Oct 5, 2026 · CT 2027 proposal pending

The state-by-state picture just got more interesting: Washington proposes a hike, Connecticut proposes a 13th straight cut — same month, opposite directions

Workers' comp remains the industry's best-performing line, with a national combined ratio near 91% for a twelfth consecutive year. Two regulators acted within weeks of each other in late September: California's commissioner took action effective September 1 to stabilize a market facing rising costs, and on September 22 Washington's L&I proposed a 4.9% average rate increase for 2027 ($1.44/week more per full-time employee). But the picture isn't uniformly worsening — on the other side, Connecticut is weighing an industry proposal for a 4.9% average decrease to voluntary loss costs (5.8% for assigned risk) for 2027, which would mark a 13th consecutive year of cost declines in that state, and manufacturing specifically got a -5.6% cut in Connecticut's 2026 filing.

What's prompting regulators to step in

  • Cumulative trauma claims — repetitive stress injuries and musculoskeletal disorders built up over time rather than a single incident — are named as a specific, growing pressure on reserves in both states' reasoning.
  • Rising medical treatment and medical-legal costs, plus escalating claims-adjusting costs, are causing deteriorating accident-year combined ratios even while the headline combined ratio still looks strong.
  • Frequency is still falling industry-wide (robotics, automated safety sensors, stronger OSHA compliance) — the good news hasn't reversed, but severity and claim complexity are clearly the part of the story regulators in multiple states are now actively managing rather than letting ride.
Why it matters: This is genuinely a state-by-state conversation now, not a national one — don't assume a client's renewal will follow whatever the last headline said. The honest framing for a manufacturing client: workers' comp overall is still favorable, and manufacturing specifically keeps outperforming, but which direction a given state moves depends on that state's own claims severity and cumulative-trauma trends. Return-to-work programs and ergonomics investment are the lever a plant controls regardless of which way its state regulator is leaning.
Sources: Insurance Journal, "Connecticut Weighs 4.9% Workers' Comp Loss Cost Decrease for 2027"
   Washington State L&I, "Proposed 2027 workers' comp rate increase helps state keep up with rising costs" (Sept 22, 2026)
Cyber Updated Oct 5, 2026 · JLR attack cost estimate updated

Jaguar Land Rover's ransomware attack is now being called the most economically damaging cyberattack in UK history — and it's a manufacturing story, not a data-breach story

If you need one example that makes manufacturing cyber risk concrete for a client, this is it. A ransomware attack on Jaguar Land Rover in early September shut down production for the better part of a month, with the cost to JLR itself now estimated at $1.7–2.4 billion. The UK's Cyber Monitoring Centre rated it a Category 3 systemic event, estimating a £1.9 billion total economic impact and more than 5,000 affected organizations — most of them small and mid-size suppliers up and down JLR's supply chain — and called it the most economically damaging cyberattack in UK history, surpassing the 2017 WannaCry outbreak. In late September, a similar pattern hit Asahi Group Holdings, the Japanese brewery and food giant: a Qilin-linked attack caused production and logistics delays severe enough to create retail shortages of the company's beers.

Why this is squarely an OT/production problem, not an IT one

  • Industrial Cyber reports manufacturing now accounts for 72% of Q3 2026 ransomware cases across all industrial sectors tracked — an even sharper concentration than the already-dominant 22% share of all-industry disclosures reported earlier this year.
  • The JLR incident's systemic rating specifically reflects how many smaller suppliers were knocked offline by one attack on a single large manufacturer — a direct illustration of why contingent business interruption coverage (Lesson 6) matters as much for a Tier 2/3 supplier's own policy as for the prime contractor's.
  • A new ransomware variant, "Settra," has been observed specifically in retail and manufacturing attacks, using remote monitoring/management (RMM) tools, recovery-disruption techniques, and "bring your own vulnerable driver" (BYOVD) methods to defeat endpoint security — a sign attackers are actively engineering around manufacturers' existing defenses.
Why it matters: JLR is the answer the next time a client asks "does this really happen to companies like us" — it's a direct competitor-scale illustration of a monthlong production shutdown with a multi-billion-dollar price tag and thousands of secondary victims. Confirm the policy's business interruption trigger explicitly covers OT/production outages, and for any manufacturer who supplies a larger OEM, ask directly whether they've modeled what happens to their own revenue if that OEM's plant goes dark for a month — that's exactly what just happened to 5,000+ companies in the UK alone.
Sources: Dark Reading, "Threat Landscape Grows Increasingly Dangerous for Manufacturers"
   Industrial Cyber, "Ransomware surge intensifies across industrial sectors, with manufacturing accounting for 72% of Q3 cases"
Tariffs & Supply Chain Updated Oct 5, 2026 · Mexico talks developing

While the US-Canada tariff fight keeps escalating, a US-Mexico deal to cut steel/aluminum tariffs is reportedly taking shape

The tariff picture that was already pushing up replacement costs earlier this year has escalated further on one front while potentially easing on another. Since April 6, 2026, amended Section 232 tariffs impose a 50% tariff on raw copper, steel, and aluminum, a flat 25% tariff on derivative products containing more than 15% covered metal, and a reduced 10% tariff on derivative products made using U.S.-origin metal. Canada's counter-tariffs, raised to match the U.S. at 50% effective September 8, remain in place and Canadian steel producers are publicly describing the squeeze as painful. Canada supplied 60% of all U.S. unwrought aluminum imports in the first half of 2026 — there is no easy alternate source at that volume. Separately, as of late September, Mexican officials say they're growing confident about a possible deal with the U.S. to reduce tariffs on steel, aluminum, and light vehicles — not finalized, but worth watching for any manufacturer sourcing from Mexico as a potential Canada alternative.

Why this keeps being a live claims problem, not just a cost story

Most manufacturing property policies carry an 80%, 90%, or 100% coinsurance requirement (see Lesson 2). Every time the metals tariff structure changes — and it has now changed materially twice this year — a plant's true replacement cost moves again, while the declared value on file typically doesn't move until the next renewal. Reporting this week also flags that because U.S.-Canada steel and aluminum supply chains are so deeply integrated, materials can effectively get taxed more than once as components cross the border multiple times during processing — a compounding effect that's easy to underestimate if you're only pricing the headline tariff rate.

Why it matters: A manufacturer sourcing aluminum from Canada isn't dealing with a marginal cost increase — they're dealing with their primary supply source getting 50% more expensive at the same time cross-border processing can stack additional tariff hits. That makes this quarter's statement-of-values conversation urgent, not routine. But also flag the Mexico development as a genuine "watch this" for clients actively re-sourcing: if that deal lands, it could change the supply-diversification and contingent business interruption math (Lesson 6) faster than a typical renewal cycle would otherwise prompt a review.
Sources: Bloomberg, "US, Mexico Negotiating Deal to Cut Tariffs on Steel, Aluminum, Vehicles"
   OilPrice.com, "Canada Tariffs Deepen North America's Steel and Aluminum Squeeze"
