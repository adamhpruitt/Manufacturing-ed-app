What's actually moving the manufacturing insurance market

A curated digest of the trends and headlines shaping rates, capacity, and coverage decisions for manufacturing accounts — each entry links to its source so you can dig deeper before a renewal conversation.

Last curated: September 2026. Each entry below is a deeper dive, not just a headline — the goal is enough detail to actually use in a renewal conversation. Always confirm current figures with your carrier or wholesaler.

Property Jan 2026 renewals – Q2 2026

Property rates are falling because reinsurance capital just hit a record — here's the mechanism

Commercial property has flipped from a decade-adjacent hard market to a genuine soft one, and it isn't just carrier sentiment — it's traceable to the reinsurance layer underneath. Global reinsurance capital reached roughly $785 billion heading into the January 1, 2026 renewals, a 9% year-over-year increase, with growth concentrated in alternative capital (cat bonds, sidecars). Large insured catastrophe losses ran about 10% below the 10-year average through early December 2025. That combination — more capital chasing fewer losses — shifted negotiating leverage decisively toward buyers (cedants) at the January renewals, and that leverage has cascaded down to primary property pricing.

The numbers on the ground

  • Primary commercial property rate reductions of up to 15% across the broad market.
  • Best-in-class risks (strong loss history, good protection, non-cat-exposed) seeing cuts as high as 50%.
  • Reinsurers remain disciplined on secondary perils (severe convective storm, wildfire, flood) even as overall capacity loosens — so a manufacturer in a cat-exposed region won't see the same relief as one in a low-hazard location.
Why it matters: This is a genuinely strong renewal story, but it's uneven — a plant with secondary-peril exposure (flooding, wildfire-adjacent siting) should not expect the same 15–50% relief quoted for a "clean" risk. Use the capacity shift as leverage to market the account, but pair it with an updated statement of values (see the tariffs entry below) so a lower rate isn't quietly offset by being underinsured on the new replacement cost.
Sources: Gallagher Re, "First View: Rate Reductions Shape January 1 Renewals"
   Zywave, 2026 Commercial Insurance Midyear Market Outlook
Casualty & Auto Q4 2025 – Q1 2026

Casualty and commercial auto are moving the opposite direction from property — and manufacturers carry more of this exposure than most

While property softens, liability lines are doing the reverse. General liability rates rose 5.6% in Q4 2025 and were forecast to climb as much as 9% in Q1 2026. Umbrella and excess casualty increases are running 8–15%, with capacity from traditional markets constrained enough that many manufacturing towers now need three or four carriers instead of one or two to fill the same limit. Commercial auto is arguably worse: increases of 8–15% are being driven by a persistent shortage of qualified commercial drivers, rising bodily injury claim costs, and litigation trends discussed below.

Why this diverges so sharply from property

Property losses are largely driven by physical catastrophe frequency/severity, which has been comparatively benign and is priced by reinsurers with a multi-year, data-heavy view. Casualty losses are driven by litigation — jury behavior, venue selection, litigation funding, and social inflation — which doesn't respond to more capital the way a catastrophe model does. More capacity doesn't make juries award less.

Why it matters: Don't let a friendly property renewal number create a false "the market is soft" narrative with a manufacturing client. Casualty and auto are the lines with the most severe long-tail, worst-case exposure for a manufacturer, and they're the ones getting more expensive and harder to place, not less.
Source: Aon, "2026 P&C Outlook: Navigating Volatility, Unlocking Growth"
Casualty & Litigation 2025 data, reported through Sept 2026

Nuclear verdicts jumped 40.7% in 2025 — and product liability is one of the sharpest edges

"Nuclear verdict" is industry shorthand for a jury award of $10 million or more (a "thermonuclear" verdict tops $100 million). In 2025, nuclear verdicts across all lines rose 40.7%, with 149 verdicts totaling $25.1 billion tracked across 28 states. The trend is also spreading: 68 industries were touched by a nuclear verdict in 2026, up from 55 in 2024 and 48 in 2023 — meaning the exposure is no longer confined to the handful of sectors that used to worry about it most.

Product liability specifically

  • Product liability cases generated 29 nuclear verdicts totaling roughly $12 billion in 2025.
  • The median product liability verdict has grown from $21 million to $36 million over the past decade — a 50% increase in severity, not just a handful of outlier mega-verdicts skewing the average.
  • Representative examples from recent years illustrate the scale: a $980 million Pennsylvania jury verdict against Mitsubishi over a defective seatbelt that left a driver paralyzed, and multiple nine- and ten-figure product liability verdicts in cases involving alleged chemical exposure and defective design.
Why it matters: This is the single strongest data point for recommending a manufacturer review — and very often increase — their umbrella and excess liability limits (see Lesson 8). A primary GL limit of $1–2 million that felt adequate five years ago is now a rounding error against a realistic worst-case product liability verdict, and the fact that 68 industries were hit in 2026 means "that doesn't happen in our sector" is a weaker argument than it used to be.
Sources: Insurance Journal, "Nuclear Verdicts Go Boom, Increase 40.7% in 2025"

Workers' Comp 2026 NCCI filings

Manufacturing keeps beating the workers' comp market average — the state-level filings show why, and where the cracks are

Workers' comp remains the industry's best-performing line, with a national combined ratio near 91% for a twelfth consecutive year. NCCI's approved 2026 loss cost filings are expected to reduce written premiums by an average of roughly 5.0% nationally, and manufacturing is outperforming even that: Connecticut's 2026 filing shows manufacturing specifically getting a -5.6% loss cost reduction against an overall state decrease of -3.8%, and New Hampshire just recorded its 15th consecutive year of workers' comp loss cost declines.

What's driving it, and what isn't fixed

  • Frequency is down industry-wide, credited to robotics/cobots reducing manual material handling, automated safety sensors, and stronger OSHA compliance programs.
  • Severity is a different story: per Risk & Insurance reporting, workers' comp premium is dipping while claim severity climbs — medical cost inflation and wage growth mean the claims that do happen cost more per claim even as there are fewer of them.
  • NCCI is moving to three-decimal-place precision on loss costs and rates starting with 2026 filings, specifically to be more responsive to these diverging frequency/severity trends state by state.
Why it matters: Falling rates are a legitimate, positive renewal talking point — but don't let it be the whole conversation. A manufacturer with rising claim severity (an aging workforce, more complex injuries, higher medical spend per claim) can still see their own experience mod move the wrong way even while the state loss cost trend is favorable. Return-to-work programs and safety investment remain the lever that protects against that.
Sources: WorkCompWire, "NH Workers' Compensation Loss Costs Decline for 15th Consecutive Year"
   Risk & Insurance, "Workers' Compensation Remains Profitable as Premium Dips and Severity Climbs"
Cyber 2025 incident data, Q1 2026 update

Manufacturing is now the single most-targeted industry for ransomware — and the attack pattern has shifted to production, not just data

Ransomware incidents targeting manufacturing rose 56% in 2025, climbing from 937 to 1,466 tracked incidents, making it the most-targeted sector globally — ahead of healthcare and financial services. The average ransom demand against a manufacturer reached $1.16 million in 2025, more than double the prior year. Per Dragos's industrial ransomware analysis, 75% of cases disrupted operations and 25% caused a full site shutdown. That matters because unplanned downtime on an automated assembly line can run $2.4 million per hour — meaning the business interruption cost of an attack routinely dwarfs the ransom itself.

Why manufacturing specifically, and who's behind it

  • Legacy OT: many plants run industrial control systems 15–25 years old, engineered for uptime and reliability, not security, and originally protected by network "air gaps" that have eroded as plants connect OT to IT for efficiency.
  • Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS): platforms like those run by the Qilin and Akira groups (the highest-volume actors against industrial targets in 2026) have lowered the technical bar for attackers.
  • Supply chain compromise is the fastest-growing attack vector, with incidents doubling in 2025 — attackers get in through a vendor's VPN, credential, or remote-access tunnel rather than attacking the manufacturer directly.
  • A notable December 2025 incident: electronics manufacturer Luxshare Precision Industry was hit by the Ransomhouse group, resulting in theft of sensitive client data — illustrating that even large, well-resourced global manufacturers are current targets, not just small shops.
Why it matters: If you're still quoting cyber for a manufacturer as a generic data-breach product, you're pricing the wrong risk. Confirm the policy's business interruption trigger explicitly covers OT/production outages (not only IT data breaches), check whether contingent BI extends to a compromised vendor or supplier, and size the limit against a realistic $2.4M/hour downtime figure, not just the ransom demand.
Sources: Industrial Cyber, "Manufacturing absorbs 56% ransomware surge of global attacks in 2025"
   Dragos, Industrial Ransomware Analysis for Q1 2026
Tariffs & Supply Chain Jan 2026 PPI data

Tariffs have pushed replacement costs up double digits — and the coinsurance math is unforgiving if declared values don't keep up

Producer price data through January 2026 showed steep, materially different increases by material: aluminum mill shapes up 33% year-over-year, steel mill products up 20.7%, and copper/brass mill shapes up 15.7%. Overall construction and equipment replacement costs are running roughly 8% higher across the board, with steel-intensive rebuilds up 15–25% versus early-2025 pricing.

Why this is a live claims problem, not just a cost story

Most manufacturing property policies carry an 80%, 90%, or 100% coinsurance requirement (see Lesson 2). If a plant's declared building/equipment value was accurate a year ago but hasn't been updated for a 15–33% jump in material costs, the insured is now carrying less than the required percentage of actual replacement value at the moment of loss — which triggers a proportional penalty on any claim payment, even a small, partial one. A policy that looked fully insured at last year's renewal can be quietly underinsured today without a single change to the declared limit.

Separately, the Allianz Risk Barometer 2026 found that 49% of manufacturers are actively renegotiating or diversifying their supply chains in response to tariffs and trade uncertainty. Reworked, longer, or more geographically dispersed supply chains generally mean longer lead times for replacement parts after an equipment failure — stretching the "period of restoration" (Lesson 6) and increasing the business income cost of a covered loss.

Why it matters: Every manufacturing property renewal right now should include a fresh statement of values reflecting current material costs, not a rolled-over number from last year — and every business interruption/equipment breakdown conversation should ask whether the client's actual parts lead times have gotten longer, since that directly affects whether the policy's period of restoration and business income limits are still adequate.
Sources: Grit Insurance, "How Tariffs Are Driving Up Construction Costs and What It Means for Your Insurance"
   Beinsure, "New US-Canada Tariffs Raise Claims Costs and Insurance Exposures"
