What's actually moving the manufacturing insurance market

A curated digest of the trends and headlines shaping rates, capacity, and coverage decisions for manufacturing accounts — each entry links to its source so you can dig deeper before a renewal conversation.

Last curated: September 21, 2026. Each entry below is a deeper dive, not just a headline — the goal is enough detail to actually use in a renewal conversation. Always confirm current figures with your carrier or wholesaler.

Property Updated Sept 21, 2026 · Q2 2026 data

Property softening just hit a fifth straight quarter — the calmest commercial market since 2017, but rebuild costs haven't gotten the memo

The property soft market isn't leveling off — it's deepening. Commercial property pricing fell 7.1% in Q1 2026, the steepest quarterly drop on record at the time, and then fell another 8.1% in Q2 — the fifth consecutive quarterly decrease. Zoom out and overall commercial insurance pricing (all lines blended) rose just 0.2% through the first half of 2026, the calmest the market has been since 2017. Clean risks outside catastrophe zones are seeing cuts up to 10%; cat-exposed accounts are seeing real but smaller relief.

The catch: replacement cost is still climbing

Commercial reconstruction costs rose 4.4% year-over-year nationally, with some states running above 7% — meaning premium and replacement cost are now moving in opposite directions. A rate quote that looks like a win on the declarations page can still leave a plant underinsured if the statement of values wasn't updated to match (see the Tariffs entry below for why rebuild costs specifically are still under pressure).

Why it matters: This is a genuinely strong renewal story to bring a manufacturing client — five straight quarters of softening is a real, usable data point. But don't let a falling rate substitute for an updated statement of values. A lower premium on an outdated building/equipment valuation just means the coinsurance penalty (Lesson 2) is quietly getting worse even as the bill gets smaller.
Sources: Insurance Business, "US property market outlook 2026: Competitive rates and emerging opportunities"
   Beancount, "Commercial Property Insurance Just Got Cheaper" (Sept 9, 2026)
Casualty & Auto Updated Sept 21, 2026 · Q2 2026 data

Casualty hardening is decelerating industry-wide — but commercial auto is still the one line moving the wrong way

The broad casualty trend has shifted from "still hardening" to "hardening, but slowing." WTW's Commercial Lines Insurance Pricing Survey shows overall U.S. commercial rates up just 0.5% in Q2 2026, down sharply from +2.5% in Q1 and +3.8% in the same quarter last year. General liability specifically rose 4.5% in Q2, down from 6.1% in Q1 — still climbing, but at a visibly slower pace. Large accounts saw an overall price decrease for the first time since 2017.

Auto is the exception, not the rule

Commercial auto rose 5.8% in early 2026 — the only major line that went up while overall commercial premiums fell 1.2% in that same snapshot. More recent monthly data (Ivans Index) shows some cooling within the trend — auto rates at 3.58% in August, down from 4.03% in July — but auto remains the outlier still moving up while most of the market moves down. The underlying drivers haven't changed: a persistent shortage of qualified commercial drivers, rising bodily injury claim costs, and the litigation dynamics covered in the next entry.

Why it matters: The "casualty is getting worse every quarter" story from earlier in 2026 needs an update — broad casualty hardening is decelerating, which is a real, current data point for a renewal conversation. But don't let that soften your auto conversation: it's still the one line bucking the trend, and it's the line most manufacturers with a delivery or field-service fleet actually carry meaningful exposure in.
Sources: Insurance Journal, "WTW, Ivans Reports Signal More Commercial Rate Moderation" (Sept 14, 2026)
   Beinsure, "US commercial insurance prices rise 0.5% in Q2 2026, WTW says"
Casualty & Litigation Updated Sept 21, 2026 · Q2 2026 verdicts

New Q2 2026 verdicts show litigation funding pushing awards past primary limits and into the umbrella tower

"Nuclear verdict" is industry shorthand for a jury award of $10 million or more (a "thermonuclear" verdict tops $100 million). The full-year 2025 trend was already stark — verdicts up 40.7%, with 149 verdicts totaling $25.1 billion across 28 states, and 68 industries touched in 2026 (up from 55 in 2024) — and fresh Q2 2026 case data shows the pattern accelerating, not correcting.

Two verdicts from this year illustrate the scale

  • A jury awarded the family of a woman killed by a counterfeit airbag a combined $603 million ($360M punitive + $243M compensatory) against a China-based manufacturer.
  • A Livingston County jury found a motorcycle's brake system defective and awarded $120 million in punitive damages to each of two plaintiffs, for a total verdict over $287 million.
  • Analysts specifically flag third-party litigation funding as an accelerant — it's associated with longer settlement times and higher-severity outcomes, with verdicts "increasingly piercing primary limits and reaching into excess and umbrella coverage."
Why it matters: "Piercing primary limits and reaching into excess and umbrella coverage" is a direct, quotable reason to review tower adequacy (Lesson 8) — this isn't hypothetical, it's what carriers and analysts are seeing happen in verdicts handed down this year. A primary GL limit of $1–2 million is not a meaningful defense against a $287M or $603M verdict; the umbrella/excess tower is doing all the real work, and it needs to be sized for that reality.
Sources: Enlyte, "The Largest Nuclear Verdicts in Q2 2026: Social Inflation Continues"
   Insurance Journal, "Nuclear Verdicts Go Boom, Increase 40.7% in 2025"
Workers' Comp Updated Sept 21, 2026 · CA action effective Sept 1

California regulators just intervened on workers' comp costs — a signal the favorable cycle has limits, even if manufacturing is still winning

Workers' comp remains the industry's best-performing line, with a national combined ratio near 91% for a twelfth consecutive year, and manufacturing keeps outperforming even that: Connecticut's 2026 NCCI filing shows manufacturing specifically getting a -5.6% loss cost reduction. But a new development is worth flagging: California's insurance commissioner took direct regulatory action, with a new rate taking effect September 1, 2026, explicitly aimed at "maintaining a stable workers' compensation market amid rising costs."

What's prompting regulators to step in

  • Cumulative trauma claims — repetitive stress injuries and musculoskeletal disorders built up over time rather than a single incident — are named as a specific, growing pressure on reserves.
  • Rising medical treatment and medical-legal costs, plus escalating claims-adjusting costs, are causing deteriorating accident-year combined ratios even while the headline combined ratio still looks strong.
  • Frequency is still falling industry-wide (robotics, automated safety sensors, stronger OSHA compliance) — the good news hasn't reversed, but severity and claim complexity are clearly the part of the story insurers and regulators are now actively managing.
Why it matters: Manufacturing is still in a genuinely favorable position on workers' comp, but California acting to stabilize the market is a real signal that the multi-year run of easy rate decreases isn't unconditional everywhere. For clients in states with growing cumulative-trauma exposure (older workforce, repetitive-motion roles), don't assume next year's renewal looks like this year's — return-to-work programs and ergonomics investment are the lever that keeps a plant on the good side of that trend.
Sources: California Department of Insurance, "Commissioner Lara takes action to maintain stable workers' compensation market amid rising costs"
   Risk Placement Services, "2026 US Workers' Compensation Market Outlook"
Cyber Updated Sept 21, 2026 · Jan–Jul 2026 data

Manufacturing ransomware has already blown past all of 2024 — with a live example this month

Manufacturing remains the single most-targeted sector for ransomware globally, and the 2026 pace is outrunning last year's record. Tracked incidents against manufacturers hit 1,183 victims through just the first seven months of 2026 — up 39.7% year-over-year and already surpassing the full-year total for 2024. European targets specifically grew 85% in the same window, showing this isn't a US-only trend.

The threat actor landscape has shifted since last update

  • Current leaderboard: Qilin, followed by "The Gentlemen" — a group that only surfaced in September 2025 and has already claimed 142 victims, the #2 spot — then Akira, DragonForce, and INC Ransom. The speed at which a brand-new group climbed to #2 shows how low the barrier to entry has become.
  • A live example this month: Aebi Schmidt, a Switzerland-based manufacturer of airport and road maintenance vehicles, had operations disrupted after a ransomware infection — a concrete, current illustration that this isn't an abstract statistic.
  • Ransomware groups are increasingly exploiting the operational shutdown itself as leverage — the disruption to a manufacturer's supply chain is the pressure point, not just the threat of leaked data.
Why it matters: If a client asks "does this really happen to companies like us," Aebi Schmidt is this month's answer, not a hypothetical. Confirm the policy's business interruption trigger explicitly covers OT/production outages (not only IT data breaches), and don't assume last year's threat-actor briefing is still accurate — the group most likely to hit a given account has probably changed since you last checked.
Sources: Industrial Cyber, "Black Kite: Manufacturing ransomware surged in 2026, spreading beyond the US"
   SecurityWeek, "Ransomware Attacks on Manufacturers Surge as Supply Chain Risk Grows"
Tariffs & Supply Chain Updated Sept 21, 2026 · Canada tariffs effective Sept 8

Tariffs escalated again in 2026 — new Section 232 rates plus fresh Canadian retaliation just weeks old

The tariff picture that was already pushing up replacement costs earlier this year has escalated further. Since April 6, 2026, amended Section 232 tariffs impose a 50% tariff on raw copper, steel, and aluminum, a flat 25% tariff on derivative products containing more than 15% covered metal, and a reduced 10% tariff on derivative products made using U.S.-origin metal. Then, effective September 8, 2026 — barely two weeks before this update — Canada imposed retaliatory tariffs up to 50% on more than 300 U.S. steel and aluminum products, including flat-rolled steel and aluminum bar stock, after trade talks broke down.

Why this keeps being a live claims problem, not just a cost story

Most manufacturing property policies carry an 80%, 90%, or 100% coinsurance requirement (see Lesson 2). Every time the metals tariff structure changes — and it has now changed materially twice this year — a plant's true replacement cost moves again, while the declared value on file typically doesn't move until the next renewal. That gap is exactly what triggers a coinsurance penalty on a claim, even a small partial one, and it's actively widening rather than settling into a stable number agents can just plug in once.

Why it matters: This is not a "set it and forget it" statement-of-values conversation anymore — the underlying tariff structure has moved twice in 2026 alone (April and September), and a manufacturer sourcing steel or aluminum from Canada specifically needs their replacement cost estimate revisited now, not at the next scheduled renewal. Pair this with the equipment breakdown and business interruption conversations (Lessons 5–6): longer, more expensive sourcing also means longer periods of restoration after a loss.
Sources: Manufacturing Dive, "Canada targets US steel and aluminum as part of retaliatory tariffs"
