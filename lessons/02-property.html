Commercial Property Insurance

9 min read · 5-question knowledge check

For most manufacturers, the property line is where the real dollars sit: buildings, production equipment, raw materials, and finished goods on hand. Getting valuation and coverage forms right here prevents the most common (and most painful) claim disputes: being underinsured after a loss.

What's being insured

  • Building — the structure itself, including permanently installed fixtures and machinery that's part of the building (like an HVAC or sprinkler system).
  • Business Personal Property (BPP) — production machinery, tools, furniture, and equipment owned by the business.
  • Stock — raw materials, work-in-process, and finished goods. For manufacturers, stock values swing throughout the year and often need a reporting form to track peak values accurately.
  • Improvements and betterments — if the manufacturer leases its space, this covers what they've built into someone else's building.

Valuation methods

How a loss is settled depends heavily on the valuation basis chosen at the time of purchase:

  • Replacement Cost Value (RCV) — pays to replace damaged property with new property of like kind, no deduction for depreciation. This is the standard choice for manufacturing equipment.
  • Actual Cash Value (ACV) — replacement cost minus depreciation. Cheaper premium, but can leave a big gap on older machinery.
  • Functional Replacement Cost — pays to replace with functionally equivalent (not identical) property, sometimes used for older buildings or obsolete equipment that would otherwise be uninsurable at true replacement cost.

Why this matters for you as a producer

Under-reported stock and equipment values are the single most common gap on a manufacturing property policy. Coinsurance penalties can slash a claim payment even on a covered loss if the insured hasn't kept limits current with replacement cost. Rising material costs from tariffs on steel, aluminum, and copper mean last year's declared values may already be stale — see the Market Updates page for current numbers.

Causes-of-loss forms

Property forms come in three tiers of breadth:

  • Basic Form — a short, named list of perils (fire, lightning, explosion, etc.).
  • Broad Form — adds perils like falling objects, weight of ice/snow, and water damage from plumbing.
  • Special Form — covers all risks of physical loss unless specifically excluded. This is what most manufacturers should carry, since it's the only form broad enough to catch loss scenarios unique to a production environment.

Note that standard property forms — even Special Form — exclude mechanical breakdown and most flood and earthquake exposure. Those require separate coverage, which you'll cover in Lesson 5 and later in this course.

Coinsurance

Most commercial property policies include a coinsurance clause (commonly 80%, 90%, or 100%) requiring the insured to carry limits equal to at least that percentage of the property's value at the time of loss. If they don't, any claim payment is reduced proportionally — even a small, partial loss. Agreed Value endorsements waive coinsurance in exchange for an accurate statement of values up front, which is often worth recommending on accounts with fluctuating inventory.

Key terms

Coinsurance
A policy requirement that the insured carry limits at or above a set percentage of the property's actual value, or absorb a penalty on claims.
Reporting Form
A property form that lets values (like fluctuating stock) be reported periodically instead of fixed at one number all year.
Agreed Value
An endorsement that suspends the coinsurance penalty as long as the statement of values was accurate when submitted.
