General Liability & Product Liability

10 min read · 5-question knowledge check

If property insurance protects what the manufacturer owns, liability insurance protects them from what happens to everyone else — visitors, contractors, and above all, the people who eventually use the product they made. For manufacturers, product liability isn't a footnote to General Liability (GL); it's often the main event.

The three buckets of a Commercial General Liability (CGL) policy

  • Premises and operations — injuries or damage happening at the manufacturer's location or arising from their ongoing operations (a visitor slips in the lobby, a delivery driver is struck by a forklift).
  • Products-completed operations — injury or damage caused by a product after it has left the manufacturer's control. This is the coverage that responds when a defective part fails at a customer's facility years later.
  • Personal and advertising injury — covers things like libel, slander, or copyright infringement in advertising — a smaller exposure for most manufacturers but still part of the standard form.

Why product liability drives the manufacturing GL conversation

A product manufactured today can generate a claim a decade from now, long after the sale is closed and the customer relationship has moved on. Courts increasingly hold manufacturers to a strict liability standard for defective products, meaning a plaintiff often doesn't need to prove negligence — only that the product was defective and caused harm. Combined with rising jury verdicts (see the Market Updates page for current nuclear verdict trends), this makes products-completed operations limits one of the first things to review on any manufacturing account.

Why this matters for you as a producer

General liability limits that looked adequate five years ago may be dangerously thin today. Verdict sizes in product liability cases have grown sharply, and a policy written with yesterday's limits can leave a manufacturer exposed well beyond what their umbrella was designed to absorb. This is one of the strongest reasons to review limits — not just premium — at every renewal.

Common exclusions and endorsements to know

  • Contractual liability — many manufacturing supply contracts require the manufacturer to indemnify a customer; standard CGL provides limited contractual liability coverage, but broader "insured contract" language matters.
  • Recall expense — standard CGL typically excludes the cost of a product recall itself (as opposed to injury caused by the product). This is often added by endorsement or written as a separate product recall policy.
  • Additional insured endorsements — large customers frequently require the manufacturer to add them as an additional insured, which needs to be reviewed for scope, not just rubber-stamped.
  • Work performed by subcontractors — if the manufacturer uses contract manufacturers or toll processors, coverage for their work needs to be confirmed, not assumed.

Occurrence vs. claims-made

Manufacturing GL is almost always written on an occurrence form, which responds based on when the injury or damage happened, regardless of when the claim is filed. This matters enormously given how long a products-completed operations claim can take to surface — a claims-made form (common in professional liability) would leave old exposures uncovered once the policy lapses.

Key terms

Products-Completed Operations
Coverage for bodily injury or property damage arising from a product after it has left the manufacturer's possession, or from completed work.
Strict Liability
A legal standard holding a manufacturer liable for a defective product regardless of fault or negligence.
Occurrence Form
A liability policy form that responds based on when the injury/damage occurred, not when the claim is reported.
Nuclear Verdict
Industry term for a jury award generally over $10 million, often disproportionate to the actual damages, driven by social inflation.
