Umbrella & Excess Liability

8 min read · 5-question knowledge check

A manufacturer's underlying GL, auto, and employers' liability limits are rarely enough on their own to absorb a catastrophic product liability verdict. Umbrella and excess liability policies exist to sit on top of those underlying policies, and for manufacturers, they're not a luxury add-on — they're often the most important layer in the whole program.

Umbrella vs. excess: what's the difference

  • Umbrella liability provides both additional limits above scheduled underlying policies and broader coverage than those underlying policies in some cases, potentially responding to certain claims the underlying policy wouldn't (subject to a self-insured retention).
  • Excess liability simply provides additional limits above the underlying policy on a "follow form" basis — it mirrors the underlying policy's terms rather than broadening them.

Many manufacturing programs use umbrella as the first layer above underlying GL/auto/employers' liability, then stack excess layers above that to build a full liability tower.

Why manufacturers need high limits

Product liability nuclear verdicts in 2025 alone totaled roughly $12 billion across product liability suits, with median verdict size in these cases having grown 50% over the past decade. A manufacturer whose primary GL policy caps out at $1 million per occurrence has, on its own, almost no ability to absorb a verdict of that scale. Umbrella and excess limits are what actually keep a single lawsuit from threatening the company's survival.

Why this matters for you as a producer

Because casualty lines — including umbrella/excess — are currently hardening even while property softens, capacity is more constrained and pricing has moved up materially. This means a manufacturer that hasn't reviewed their tower structure in a few years may be paying more for less capacity than they realize, or worse, may have gaps where a carrier reduced limits at renewal without the account fully noticing. This is a genuinely valuable conversation to initiate, not just respond to.

Attachment points and underlying requirements

Umbrella and excess carriers require specific minimum limits on underlying policies (GL, auto, employers' liability) before they'll attach. If underlying limits are reduced or a policy lapses without the umbrella carrier's knowledge, coverage gaps called "gap exposure" or "drop-down" issues can occur. Verifying that underlying limits still meet the umbrella's attachment requirements at every renewal is one of the most important, and most commonly skipped, review steps.

Building the tower

For larger manufacturers, especially those with significant product liability exposure or export sales, a liability tower might include a primary umbrella layer plus two or three excess layers from different carriers, each attaching above the last. Coordinating this tower — making sure follow-form language is consistent and there are no gaps between layers — is specialized work, and it's exactly where an experienced producer adds visible value beyond a rate comparison.

Key terms

Follow Form
Excess policy language that mirrors the terms and conditions of the underlying policy rather than creating its own broader terms.
Self-Insured Retention (SIR)
An amount the insured must pay out of pocket before umbrella coverage responds, similar in effect to a deductible.
Attachment Point
The dollar amount at which an excess or umbrella layer begins to respond, sitting directly above the underlying limit.
Liability Tower
The full structure of primary and stacked excess/umbrella layers that together make up a company's total liability limits.
